The Rise of Online Gambling: What’s at Stake?

Read report ↗

Executive summary

This paper estimates the overall financial impact of online sports betting in the United States by combining two connected models. First, we estimate how much disposable income individuals have after accounting for taxes and major living expenses. Second, we simulate how individuals with different demographic characteristics might bet over time, given that disposable income and a modeled risk tolerance. By repeating this process for many representative individuals and scaling the results to the national level, we estimate both total wagering volume and total net losses incurred by bettors.

The goal is to answer three central questions: how disposable income differs across demographic groups, how that income translates into betting behavior, and what those patterns imply when expanded to the entire country. The simulation reflects differences in age, gender, income, marital status, housing status, and region, allowing individuals to bet at different intensities and to stop once losses or gains reach preset thresholds. Because sports outcomes are uncertain, we run the Markov process model many times and examine average results rather than relying on a single run.

Our findings show that disposable income plays a central role in determining betting scale. Individuals with higher disposable income are able to place larger bets and continue longer before reaching financial limits, while those with lower disposable income tend to stop sooner. When aggregated nationally through Monte Carlo simulation, total wagering volume is large, and total net losses by bettors align closely with the assumed sportsbook hold rate. The model also indicates that a relatively small share of higher-intensity bettors accounts for a disproportionate portion of total wagering and losses, even though many people participate at modest levels.

Rather than presenting a single exact figure, we report central simulation estimates that reflect normal statistical variation in outcomes. This provides a clearer picture of likely national impact without implying false precision. While the results depend on assumptions about participation rates, independence of demographic effects, and fixed risk tolerance, the combined framework offers a coherent way to connect individual financial capacity to national-level impact. Overall, the model provides a structured estimate of how much money flows through sports betting nationally and how much bettors lose in aggregate.
